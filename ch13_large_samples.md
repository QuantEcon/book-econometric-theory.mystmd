With IID data, the joint distribution of the sample is pinned down by the common marginal of the observations. The statistical learning problem becomes one of learning the marginal. With dependent data the joint distribution is more complex. The fundamental statistical problem has to be extended as follows:

```{admonition} Problem
:label: p-fpstat

We observe a draw $\zdata$ from some joint distribution $\Pdata$, which is assumed to belong to a given class of distributions $\pP$ but is otherwise unknown. We wish to infer some features of $\Pdata$ from the observation $\zdata$.
```


Let's look at how we might tackle this problem, starting with least squares.

(ss-lslse)=
## Large Sample Least Squares
Our treatment of least squares in this chapter permits dependent data but at the same time requires that the sample size is large. Large samples allow us to drop some of the strict parametric assumptions on the error term we made when we considered finite sample inference (see, e.g., [assumption %s](#a-bolsg)). In this sense, the theory developed below is also useful for cross-sectional environments with no correlation between observations.

(ss-sua)=
### Setup and Assumptions
Let's go back to the linear model assumption of [chapter 12](#c-tcolse). That is, we assume that our data $(y_1, \boldx_1), \ldots, (y_T, \boldx_T)$ are generated by the linear model

$$
y_t = \boldx_t^\T \boldbeta + u_t,
    \qquad t = 1, \ldots, T,
$$ (eq-lrtsc)

where $\boldbeta$ is a $K$-vector of unknown coefficients, and $u_t$ is an unobservable shock. Observations are indexed by $t$ rather than $n$ to remind us that observations are dependent. The sample size will be denoted by $T$.

We let $\boldy$ be the $T \times 1$ vector of observed outputs, so that $y_t$ is the $t$th element of $\boldy$, and $\boldu$ be the vector of shocks, so that $u_t$ is the $t$th element of $\boldu$. We let $\boldX$ be the $T \times K$ matrix $\boldX := (x_{tk})$, where $1 \leq t \leq T$ and $1 \leq k \leq K$.

We will estimate the parameter vector $\boldbeta$ via least squares. The OLS estimate is therefore unchanged at

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\hboldbeta_T 
    = \left[ \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \boldx_t \boldx_t^\T \right]^{-1} 
            \cdot \; \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \boldx_t y_t
```

(Recall {eq}`eq-seeo0`.) The expression for the sampling error in {eq}`eq-hg` can be expanded into sums to obtain

$$
\hboldbeta_T - \boldbeta 
    = \left[ \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \boldx_t \boldx_t^\T \right]^{-1} 
        \cdot \; \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \boldx_t u_t
$$ (eq-seeo)

In this section we drop the exogeneity assumption $\EE[\boldu \given \boldX] = \boldzero$ ([assumption %s](#a-bols)) because it excludes too many dynamic models. For example, we know from {ref}`ss-eb` that exogeneity fails when we try to estimate the simple AR(1) model $y_{t+1} = \beta y_t + u_{t+1}$ by setting $x_t = y_{t-1}$, thereby producing the regression model

$$
y_t = \beta x_t + u_t,
    \qquad t=1,\ldots,T
$$ (eq-ar1reg)

For this specification of {eq}`eq-lrtsc`, the regressor is correlated with lagged values of the shock.

```{prf:assumption}
:label: a-rtsc

The matrix $\boldX$ is full column rank with probability one and the sequence $\{\boldx_t\}$ is stationary. Moreover

(i) $\Sigmax := \EE [ \boldx_t \boldx_t^\T ]$ exists and is positive definite, and

(ii) the sequence $\{\boldx_t\}$ satisfies $\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \boldx_t \boldx_t^\T \toprob \Sigmax$ as $T \to \infty$.
```


The full column rank condition is the same as [assumption %s](#a-fr) and ensures that expressions like the matrix inverse in {eq}`eq-seeo` make sense. Condition (i) is borrowed from the identical condition given in {eq}`eq-smx` and ensures that the limit in {eq}`eq-seeo` exists. Condition (ii) gives convergence.

````{prf:example}
:label: eg-ztar2

Let $\{x_t\}$ be the Markov process in [example %s](#eg-ztar). To repeat,

```{math}
:enumerated: false

x_{t+1} = a |x_t| + (1 - a^2)^{1/2} w_{t+1}  
        \quad \text{with} \quad
        -1 < a < 1
        \quad \text{and} \quad
        \{w_t\} \iidsim \nN(0, 1)
```

As discussed in [example %s](#eg-ztar), the model has a unique, globally stable stationary distribution $\pi_\infty$. If $\lL(x_0) = \pi_\infty$, then the process $\{x_t\}$ is stationary (see [fact %s](#fa-idmp)) and all of the conditions in [assumption %s](#a-rtsc) are satisfied (see [ex. %s](#ex-chrtsc)).
````


```{prf:assumption}
:label: a-stsc

The shocks $\{u_t\}$ are IID. Moreover

(i) $\EE[u_t] = 0$ and $\EE[u_t^2] = \sigma^2$ for all $t$, and

(ii) $u_t$ is independent of $\boldx_1, \boldx_2,\ldots,\boldx_t$ for all $t$.
```


{prf:ref}`a-stsc` permits dependence between current shocks and future regressors. This is desirable in a time series setting, since current shocks usually feed into future state variables.

```{prf:example}
:label: eg-ar1lsols

In the AR(1) regression {eq}`eq-ar1reg`, [assumption %s](#a-stsc) holds whenever the shock process $\{u_t\}$ is IID, because the contemporaneous and lagged regressors $x_1, \ldots, x_t$ are equal to the lagged state variables $y_0, \ldots, y_{t-1}$, which in turn are functions of only $y_0$ and $u_1,\ldots,u_{t-1}$, and therefore independent of $u_t$.
```


One consequence of [assumption %s](#a-stsc) is that

$$
\EE[ u_s u_t \given \boldx_1, \ldots,\boldx_t]  
    = 
    \begin{cases}
        & \sigma^2 \quad \text{if} \quad s = t \\ 
        & 0 \quad \;\; \text{if} \quad s < t 
    \end{cases}
$$ (eq-cstsc)

The proof is an exercise ([ex. %s](#ex-cstsc)).

A useful implication of [assumptions %s](#a-rtsc) and [%s](#a-stsc) is that linear functions of $\{\boldx_t u_t\}$ form a martingale difference sequence (MDS). This will allow us to apply the sample path results in [theorem %s](#t-mdclt).

```{prf:lemma}
:label: l-xumds

If [assumptions %s](#a-rtsc) and [%s](#a-stsc) both hold, then, for any constant vector $\bolda \in \RR^K$, the sequence $\{m_t\}$ defined by $m_t = \bolda^\T \boldx_t u_t$ is

(i) stationary with $\EE[m_t^2] = \sigma^2  \bolda^\T \Sigmax \bolda$ for all $t$, and

(ii) an MDS with respect to the filtration defined by

$$
\fF_t := \{\boldx_1,\ldots,\boldx_t, \boldx_{t+1}, u_1, \ldots, u_t\}
$$ (eq-deffil)
```


````{prf:proof}
:nonumber:

First let's check part (i). That $\{m_t\}$ is stationary follows from the assumption that $\{u_t\}$ and $\{\boldx_t\}$ are stationary. Regarding the second moment $\EE[m_1^2]$, we have

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\EE[m_1^2] 
        = \EE [ \EE[ u_1^2 (\bolda^\T \boldx_1)^2 \given \boldx_1]]
        = \EE [ (\bolda^\T \boldx_1)^2 \EE[ u_1^2 \given \boldx_1]]
```

From independence of $u_1$ and $\boldx_1$, the inner expectation is $\sigma^2$. Moreover

```{math}
:enumerated: false

(\bolda^\T \boldx_1)^2 = \bolda^\T \boldx_1 \bolda^\T \boldx_1 
            =  \bolda^\T \boldx_1 \boldx_1^\T \bolda
```

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\fore
        \EE[m_1^2] 
        = \EE [ \bolda^\T \boldx_1 \boldx_1^\T  \bolda \; \sigma^2 ]
        = \sigma^2  \bolda^\T \EE [ \boldx_1 \boldx_1^\T ] \bolda 
        = \sigma^2  \bolda^\T \Sigmax \bolda
```

To check part (ii), note that $\{m_t\}$ is adapted to $\{\fF_t\}$, since $m_t := u_t \bolda^\T \boldx_t$ is a function of variables in $\fF_t$. Moreover we have

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\EE[ m_{t+1} \given \fF_t ]
        = \EE[ u_{t+1} \bolda^\T \boldx_{t+1} \given \fF_t ]
        = \bolda^\T \boldx_{t+1} \EE[ u_{t+1}  \given \fF_t ]
        = \bolda^\T \boldx_{t+1} \EE[ u_{t+1} ]
        = 0
```

(Here the second equality follows from the fact that $\boldx_{t+1} \in \fF_t$, while the third follows from the independence in [assumption %s](#a-stsc).) This confirms that $\{m_t\}$ is an MDS with respect to $\{\fF_t\}$. ◻
````


### Consistency

Under the conditions of {ref}`ss-sua`, the OLS estimator $\hboldbeta_T$ is consistent for $\boldbeta$:

````{prf:theorem}
:label: t-cofols

If [assumptions %s](#a-rtsc) and [%s](#a-stsc) hold, then

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\hboldbeta_T \toprob \boldbeta \quad \text{as} \quad T \to \infty
```
````


````{prf:proof}
:nonumber:

It suffices to show that the expression on the right-hand side of {eq}`eq-seeo` converges in probability to $\boldzero$. As a first step, let's show that $\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \boldx_t u_t \toprob \boldzero$. In view of [fact %s](#fa-reconpro), it suffices to show that, for any $\bolda \in \RR^K$, we have

$$
\bolda^\T \left[ \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \boldx_t u_t \right]
        \toprob \bolda^\T \boldzero = 0
$$ (eq-amw)
If we define $m_t := \bolda^\T \boldx_t u_t$, then the left-hand side of {eq}`eq-amw` can be written as $T^{-1} \sum_{t=1}^T m_t$. Since $\{m_t\}$ is a stationary MDS ([lemma %s](#l-xumds)), the convergence $T^{-1} \sum_{t=1}^T m_t \toprob 0$ follows from [theorem %s](#t-mdclt).

Now let's return to the expression on the right-hand side of {eq}`eq-seeo`. By [assumption %s](#a-rtsc) and [fact %s](#fa-cmtetcv1), we see that

$$
\left[ \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \boldx_t \boldx_t^\T \right]^{-1} \toprob \Sigmax^{-1}
        \quad \text{as} \quad
        T \to \infty
$$ (eq-cttii)
Appealing to [fact %s](#fa-cmtetcv1) once more, we obtain

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\hboldbeta_T - \boldbeta 
        = \left[ \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \boldx_t \boldx_t^\T \right]^{-1} 
            \cdot \; \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T u_t \boldx_t 
            \toprob \Sigmax^{-1} \, \boldzero = \boldzero
        
```
````


#### Consistency of $\hat \sigma^2_T$

In {eq}`eq-hatsig` we estimated the error variance $\sigma^2$ using $\hat \sigma^2 := \rss / (N - K)$, with $N$ being the sample size. Here the sample size is $T$, and since $T$ is assumed to be large relative to $K$, we have $1/(T-K) \approx 1/T$. Hence in this chapter we set $\hat \sigma^2_T = \rss / T$ instead. (None of the following theory is affected if we use $\rss / (T-K)$, however.)

````{prf:theorem}
:label: t-cofhs2

If [assumptions %s](#a-rtsc) and [%s](#a-stsc) hold, then

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\hat \sigma^2_T \toprob \sigma^2
        \quad \text{as} \quad T \to \infty
```
````


````{prf:proof}
:nonumber:

By the definition of $\hat \sigma_T^2$ and the linear model assumption {eq}`eq-lrtsc`,

$$
\hat \sigma_T^2 
        = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T (y_t - \boldx_t^\T \, \hboldbeta_T)^2
        = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T 
        \left[ u_t + \boldx_t^\T \, (\boldbeta - \hboldbeta_T) \right]^2
$$ (eq-ndhs2)
Expanding out the square gives

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\hat \sigma_T^2 
        = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T u_t^2
        + 2 (\boldbeta - \hboldbeta_T)^\T \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \boldx_t u_t 
        + (\boldbeta - \hboldbeta_T)^\T 
            \left[ \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \boldx_t \boldx_t^\T \right]
         (\boldbeta - \hboldbeta_T)
```

By [assumption %s](#a-stsc) and the law of large numbers, the first term on the right-hand side converges in probability to $\sigma^2$. Hence it suffices to show that the second and third term converge in probability to zero as $T \to \infty$. These results follow from repeated applications of [fact %s](#fa-cmtetcv1) and convergence results we have already established. The details are left as an exercise. ◻
````


### Asymptotic Normality of $\hboldbeta$

Next we show that $\sqrt{T} (\hboldbeta_T - \boldbeta)$ is asymptotically normal. From this information we can develop asymptotic tests and confidence intervals.

````{prf:theorem}
:label: t-cltols

If [assumptions %s](#a-rtsc) and [%s](#a-stsc) hold, then

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\sqrt{T} (\hboldbeta_T - \boldbeta) \tod 
        \nN \left(\boldzero, \sigma^2 \Sigmaxinv \right)
        \quad \text{as} \quad T \to \infty
```
````


````{prf:proof}
:nonumber:

Expression {eq}`eq-seeo` gives

$$
\sqrt{T}(\hboldbeta_T - \boldbeta)
        = \left[ \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \boldx_t \boldx_t^\T \right]^{-1} 
        \cdot \; T^{-1/2} \sum_{t=1}^T u_t \boldx_t
$$ (eq-seeo2)
Let $\boldz$ be a random variable satisfying $\lL(\boldz) = \nN(\boldzero, \sigma^2 \Sigmax)$. Suppose we can show that

$$
T^{-1/2} \sum_{t=1}^T u_t \boldx_t \tod \boldz
        \quad \text{as} \quad T \to \infty
$$ (eq-cux)
If {eq}`eq-cux` is valid, then, applying [assumption %s](#a-rtsc) along with [fact %s](#fa-cmtetcv2), we obtain

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\sqrt{T}(\hboldbeta_T - \boldbeta)
        = \left[ \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \boldx_t \boldx_t^\T \right]^{-1} 
        \cdot \; T^{-1/2} \sum_{t=1}^T u_t \boldx_t
        \,\, \tod \,\, \Sigmaxinv \boldz
```

Clearly $\Sigmaxinv \boldz$ is Gaussian with zero mean. By symmetry of $\Sigmaxinv$ (since $\Sigmax$ is symmetric) the variance of $\Sigmaxinv \boldz$ is

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\Sigmaxinv \, \var[\boldz] \, \Sigmaxinv 
         = 
         \Sigmaxinv \, \sigma^2 \, \Sigmax  \,\Sigmaxinv 
         = 
         \sigma^2 \Sigmaxinv
```

This completes the proof of [theorem %s](#t-cltols), conditional on {eq}`eq-cux`. Let's now check that {eq}`eq-cux` is valid.

By the Cramér–Wold device ([fact %s](#fa-cmtetc)), it suffices to show that for any $\bolda \in \RR^K$ we have

$$
\bolda^\T \left[ T^{-1/2} \sum_{t=1}^T u_t \boldx_t \right] \tod \bolda^\T \boldz
$$ (eq-cux2)
Fixing $\bolda$ and letting $m_t := u_t \bolda^\T \boldx_t$, the expression on the left of {eq}`eq-cux2` can be rewritten as $T^{-1/2} \sum_{t=1}^T m_t$. Since $\lL(\boldz) = \nN(\boldzero, \sigma^2 \Sigmax)$, to establish {eq}`eq-cux2` we need to show that

$$
T^{-1/2} \sum_{t=1}^T m_t 
          \tod \nN(0, \sigma^2 \bolda^\T \Sigmax \bolda)
$$ (eq-cux3)
From [lemma %s](#l-xumds) we know that $\{m_t\}$ is stationary with $\EE[m_t^2] = \sigma^2  \bolda^\T \Sigmax \bolda$ and an MDS with respect to the filtration given in {eq}`eq-deffil`. By the martingale difference CLT, the result in {eq}`eq-cux3` holds whenever

$$
\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \EE[m_t^2 \given \fF_{t-1} ] 
        \toprob \sigma^2 \bolda^\T \Sigmax \bolda
        \quad \text{as } T \to \infty
$$ (eq-laon)
Since $\boldx_t \in \fF_{t-1}$, we have

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\EE[m_t^2 \given \fF_{t-1} ] 
        = \EE[ u_t^2 (\bolda^\T \boldx_t)^2 \given \fF_{t-1}]
        =  (\bolda^\T \boldx_t)^2 \EE[ u_t^2  \given \fF_{t-1}]
        = \sigma^2 (\bolda^\T \boldx_t)^2
```

Another way to write the last expression is $\sigma^2  \bolda^\T \boldx_t \boldx_t^\T \bolda$. The left-hand side of {eq}`eq-laon` is therefore

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \EE[m_t^2 \given \fF_{t-1} ] 
        = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T (\sigma^2  \bolda^\T \boldx_t \boldx_t^\T \bolda)
        = \sigma^2  \bolda^\T 
        \left[ 
            \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T  \boldx_t \boldx_t^\T
        \right]  \bolda
```

which converges in probability to $\sigma^2 \bolda^\T \Sigmax \bolda$ by [assumption %s](#a-rtsc) and [fact %s](#fa-cmtetcv1). This verifies {eq}`eq-laon`, completing the proof of [theorem %s](#t-cltols). ◻
````


````{prf:example}
:label: eg-olsrar1

Consider again the scalar linear Gaussian AR(1) model $x_{t+1} = a x_t + w_{t+1}$ with $|a| < 1$ and $\{w_t\}$ IID and standard normal. Let $\{x_t\}$ be stationary. As discussed in {ref}`ss-eb`, the OLS estimator of $a$ is

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\hat a_T := \frac{\boldx^\T \boldy} {\boldx^\T \boldx}
        \quad \text{where} \quad
        \boldy := (x_1,\ldots,x_T) \text{ and  } \boldx := (x_0,\ldots,x_{T-1})
```

Both [assumption %s](#a-rtsc) and [assumption %s](#a-stsc) are satisfied, so $\sqrt{T} (\hat a_T - a)$ converges in distribution to $\nN(0, \sigma^2 \Sigmaxinv)$. In this case, $\sigma^2  = 1$ because the shocks are standard normal. Furthermore $\Sigmaxinv$ reduces to $1/\EE[x_1^2]$, where the expectation is under the stationary distribution. By {eq}`eq-sdsg`, we see that the stationary distribution is $\nN(0, 1/(1-a^2))$. Hence the inverse of $\EE[x_1^2]$ is $1 - a^2$, and

$$
\sqrt{T} (\hat a_T - a) \tod \nN(0, 1 - a^2)
$$ (eq-avarrols)
````


### Large Sample Tests

In {ref}`ss-ttt` we considered the problem of testing a hypothesis about an individual coefficient $\beta_k$. Let's consider this problem again in the large sample setting. The hypothesis to be tested is

```{math}
:enumerated: false

H_0 \colon \beta_k = \beta_k^0
```

In {ref}`ss-ttt` we showed that if the error terms are normally distributed, then the expression $(\hat \beta_k - \beta_k) / \se(\hat \beta_k)$ is $t$-distributed with $N-K$ degrees of freedom. In the large sample case, we can use the CLT to show that the same statistic is asymptotically normal.[^1] The next theorem gives details.

````{prf:theorem}
:label: t-ttest2

Let [assumptions %s](#a-rtsc) and [%s](#a-stsc) hold, and let

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\se(\hat \beta_k^T) 
        := \sqrt{ \hat \sigma^2_T v_k(\boldX) }
```

Under the null hypothesis $H_0$, we have

$$
z_k^T := \frac{\hat \beta_k^T - \beta_k^0}{\se(\hat \beta_k^T)}
        \tod \nN(0,1) 
        \quad \text{as} \quad
        T \to \infty
$$ (eq-ttest2)
````


In the theorem, $\hat \sigma_T$ is as defined in {eq}`eq-ndhs2` and $v_k (\boldX)$ is the $(k,k)$th element of $(\boldX^\T \boldX)^{-1}$.

````{prf:proof}
:nonumber:

Recall from [theorem %s](#t-cltols) that $\sqrt{T} (\hboldbeta_T - \boldbeta) \tod \boldz$, where $\boldz$ is a random vector with distribution $\nN(\boldzero, \sigma^2 \Sigmaxinv)$ and $\boldbeta$ is the true parameter vector. It follows that

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\sqrt{T} (\hat \beta_k^T - \beta_k)
        = \bolde_k^\T [\sqrt{T} (\hboldbeta_T - \boldbeta)] \tod 
        \bolde_k^\T \boldz
```

The distribution of $\bolde_k^\T \boldz$ is $\nN(0, \bolde_k^\T \var[\boldz] \bolde_k) = \nN(0, \sigma^2 \bolde_k^\T \Sigmaxinv \bolde_k)$, so

$$
\frac{\sqrt{T} (\hat \beta_k^T - \beta_k)}
                {\sqrt{\sigma^2 \bolde_k^\T \Sigmaxinv \bolde_k}}
        \tod \nN(0, 1)
$$ (eq-tscno)
Applying {eq}`eq-cttii` and (v) of [fact %s](#fa-cmtetcv1),

```{math}
:enumerated: false

T v_k (\boldX)
        = 
        T \bolde_k^\T 
            (\boldX^\T\boldX)^{-1}
        \bolde_k
        =
        \bolde_k^\T 
            \left[ \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \boldx_t \boldx_t^\T \right]^{-1}
        \bolde_k
        \toprob \bolde_k^\T \Sigmax^{-1} \bolde_k
```

By [theorem %s](#t-cofhs2) we have $\hat \sigma_T^2 \toprob \sigma^2$, and hence

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\sqrt{ \hat \sigma_T^2 \, T v_k(\boldX) }
        \toprob 
        \sqrt{ \sigma^2 \bolde_k^\T \Sigmax^{-1} \bolde_k }
```

Combining this with {eq}`eq-tscno` yields

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\frac{\sqrt{T} (\hat \beta_k^T - \beta_k)}
        {\sqrt{\hat \sigma_T^2 \, T v_k(\boldX)}}
        \tod 
            \nN(0, 1)
```

Assuming $H_0$ and canceling $\sqrt{T}$ gives {eq}`eq-ttest2`. ◻
````


## MLE for Markov Processes

Let's now turn to nonlinear estimation in a time series setting, using maximum likelihood. We will cover just the foundations of building and maximizing the likelihood.

### The Likelihood Function

If we have IID observations from some common density, then, as we saw in {ref}`ss-ti`, the likelihood function for that data is the product of the marginals evaluated at the sample. If, instead, the data contains dependence, the likelihood is more complex.

One intermediate case is Markov processes, the joint distribution of which was given in {eq}`eq-jftd`. Suppose that the transition density $p_{\boldtheta}$ depends on some unknown parameter vector $\boldtheta \in \Theta$. Suppose further that the process has a unique stationary density $\pi_{\infty}^{\boldtheta}$ for all $\boldtheta$, and that $\boldx_1$ is a draw from this stationary density. Then the log-likelihood function is given by

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\ell(\boldtheta) =  \ln \pi_{\infty}^{\boldtheta}(\boldx_1) +
        \sum_{t=1}^{T-1} \ln p_{\boldtheta}(\boldx_{t+1} \given \boldx_t)
```

In practice, it is usual to drop the first term in this expression, particularly when the data size is large, since the influence of a single element is likely to be negligible. In addition, even though the stationary density is formally defined by {eq}`eq-dsdist`, for many processes, there is no known analytical expression for this density.[^2] Here we'll follow this convention and, abusing notation slightly, write

$$
\ell(\boldtheta) = \sum_{t=1}^{T-1} \ln p_{\boldtheta}(\boldx_{t+1} \given \boldx_t)
$$ (eq-likemark)

#### Example: The ARCH Case

Let's look at a relatively simple case: the ARCH model from {eq}`eq-arch`. By {eq}`eq-likemark`, the log-likelihood function is

$$
\ell(a, b) = \sum_{t=1}^{T-1} 
     \left\{ 
     - \frac{1}{2} \ln(2 \pi (a + b x_t^2)) - \frac{x_{t+1}^2}{2(a + b x_t^2)} 
        \right\}
$$ (eq-likearch)

Rearranging, dropping terms that don't depend on $a$ or $b$, and multiplying by 2 (an increasing transformation), we can rewrite this (abusing notation again) as

$$
\ell(a, b) =  - \sum_{t=1}^{T-1} 
    \left\{ \ln z_t  + \frac{x_{t+1}^2}{z_t}  \right\}
    \quad \text{where} \quad
    z_t := a + b x_t^2
$$ (eq-likearch2)

Let's run some simulations to see what this function looks like. In the simulations we will set $T=500$ and $a=b=0.5$. Thus, we imagine the situation where, unbeknown to us, the true parameter values are $a=b=0.5$, and we observe a time series $x_1,\ldots,x_{500}$ generated by these parameters. In order to estimate $a$ and $b$, we form the likelihood function {eq}`eq-likearch2`, and obtain the MLEs $\hat a$ and $\hat b$ as the vector $(\hat a, \hat b)$ that maximizes $\ell(a,b)$.

Four different simulations of $\ell$ are given in [figure %s](#f-arch_plot). In each figure, a separate data set $x_1,\ldots,x_{500}$ is generated using the true parameter values $a=b=0.5$ and the function $\ell$ in {eq}`eq-likearch2` is then plotted using contour lines and a color map. Lighter colors refer to larger values. The horizontal axis is $a$ values, and the vertical axis is $b$ values. The code for producing one of these figures (modulo randomness) is given in [listing %s](#list-arch_plot). The function `arch_like(theta, data)` represents $\ell$ in {eq}`eq-likearch2`, with `theta` corresponding to $(a,b)$ and `data` corresponding to the time series $x_1,\ldots,x_T$.

In each of the four simulations, a rough guess of the MLEs can be obtained just by looking for maximizers in the figures. For example, in simulation (a), the MLEs look to be around $\hat a = 0.44$ and $\hat b = 0.61$. To get more accurate estimates, we can use some form of analytical or numerical optimization. For this problem we don't have any analytical expressions for the MLEs because setting the two partial derivatives of $\ell$ in {eq}`eq-likearch2` to zero does not yield neat expressions for $\hat a$ and $\hat b$. However, there are many numerical routines we can use to obtain the MLEs for a given data set.

The simplest approach is to use one of `R`'s inbuilt optimization routines. For example, given the definition of `arch_like` in [listing %s](#list-arch_plot) and a sequence of observations $x_1,\ldots,x_T$ stored in a vector `xdata`, the function `arch_like` can be optimized numerically via the commands

``` r
start_theta <- c(0.65, 0.35)  # An initial guess of (a,b)
neg_like <- function(theta) {
    return(-arch_like(theta, xdata))  # xdata is the data
}
opt <- optim(start_theta, neg_like, method="BFGS")
```

Here `optim` is an built-in `R` function for numerical optimization of multivariate functions. Most built-in functions perform minimization rather than maximization and `optim` is no exception. For this reason the function that we pass to `optim` is `neg_like`, which is $-1$ times $\ell$. The first argument to `optim` is a vector of starting values (a guess of the MLEs). The last argument tells `optim` to use the BFGS routine, which is variation on the Newton–Raphson algorithm. The return value of `optim` is a list, and the approximate minimizing vector is one element of this list (called `par`).

In this particular setup, for most realizations of the data and starting values, you will find that the algorithm converges to a good approximation to the global optimizer. However, there's no guarantee that it will. In case of problems, it's useful to know how these kinds of algorithms work, and how to code up simple implementations on your own. The next section will get you started.

### The Newton–Raphson Algorithm
The Newton–Raphson algorithm is a *root-finding* algorithm. In other words, given a function $g \colon \RR \to \RR$, the algorithm searches for points $\bar s \in \RR$ such that $g(\bar s) = 0$. Any root-finding algorithm can be used to optimize differentiable functions because, for differentiable functions, interior optimizers are always roots of the objective function's first derivative.

To describe the algorithm, let's begin with the root-finding problem and then specialize to optimization. To begin, let $g \colon \RR \to \RR$, and let $s_0$ be some initial point in $\RR$ that we think (hope) is somewhere near a root. We don't know how to jump from $s_0$ straight to a root of $g$ (otherwise, there would be no problem to solve), but what we can do is move to the root of the function that forms the *tangent line* to $g$ at $s_0$. In other words, we replace $g$ with its linear approximation around $s_0$, which is given by

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\tilde g(s) := g(s_0) + g'(s_0)(s - s_0)   
    \qquad (s \in \RR)
```

and solve for the root of $\tilde g$. This point is represented as $s_1$ in [figure %s](#f-nr1d), and the value is easily seen to be $s_1 := s_0 - g(s_0)/g'(s_0)$. The point $s_1$ is taken as our next guess of the root, and the procedure is repeated, taking the tangent of $g$ at $s_1$, solving for the root, and so on. This generates a sequence of points $\{s_k\}$ satisfying

```{math}
:enumerated: false

s_{k+1} = s_k - \frac{g(s_k)}{g'(s_k)}
```

There are various results telling us that when $g$ is suitably well-behaved and $s_0$ is sufficiently close to a given root $\bar s$, then sequence $\{s_k\}$ will converge to $\bar s$.[^3]

```{figure} figures/fig_nr1d.svg
:name: f-nr1d

First step of the Newton–Raphson algorithm
```

To move from general root-finding to the specific problem of optimization, suppose now that $g \colon \RR \to \RR$ is a twice differentiable function we wish to maximize. We know that if $s^*$ is a maximizer of $g$, then $g'(s^*) = 0$. Hence it is natural to begin our search for maximizers by looking for roots to this equation. This can be done by applying the Newton–Raphson algorithm to $g'$, which yields the sequence

$$
s_{k+1} = s_k - \frac{g'(s_k)}{g''(s_k)}
$$ (eq-1dnr)

We can extend this algorithm to the multivariate case as well. Let's suppose that $g$ is a function of two arguments. In particular, suppose that $g$ is twice differentiable and $g \colon \RR^2 \to \RR$. The **gradient vector** and **Hessian** of $g$ at $(x,y) \in \RR^2$ are defined as

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\nabla g(x,y) := 
    \left( 
    \begin{array}{c}
        g'_1 (x,y) \\
        g'_2 (x,y) 
    \end{array}
    \right)
    \quad \text{and} \quad
    \nabla^2 g(x,y) := 
    \left( 
    \begin{array}{cc}
        g''_{11} (x,y) 
        & g''_{12} (x,y) 
        \\
        g''_{21} (x,y) 
        & g''_{22} (x,y) 
    \end{array}
    \right)
```

Here $g'_i$ is the first partial of $g$ with respect to its $i$th argument, $g''_{ij}$ is the second cross-partial, and so on.

By analogy with {eq}`eq-1dnr`, the Newton–Raphson algorithm for this two dimensional case is the algorithm that generates the sequence $\{(x_k, y_k)\}$ defined by

$$
(x_{k+1}, y_{k+1}) = (x_k, y_k) 
        - [\nabla^2 g(x_k, y_k)]^{-1} \nabla g(x_k, y_k)
$$ (eq-2dnr)

from some initial guess $(x_0, y_0)$.[^4]

For the sake of the exercise, let's apply this to maximization of {eq}`eq-likearch2`. Let $z_t$ be as defined in {eq}`eq-likearch2`. The first partials are then

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial a} (a,b) 
    = 
    \sum_{t=1}^{T-1}
    \left[
            \frac{x_{t+1}^2}{z_t^2} - \frac{1}{z_t}
        \right],
    \quad
    \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial b} (a,b) 
    = 
    \sum_{t=1}^{T-1}
         x_t^2 \left[ 
            \frac{x_{t+1}^2}{z_t^2} - \frac{1}{z_t}
        \right]
```

while the second partials are

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial a^2} (a,b) = 
    \sum_{t=1}^{T-1}
    \left[
            \frac{1}{z_t^2} - 2 \frac{x_{t+1}^2}{z_t^3}  
        \right],
    \quad
    \frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial b^2} (a,b) 
    = 
    \sum_{t=1}^{T-1} x_t^4
    \left[
            \frac{1}{z_t^2} - 2 \frac{x_{t+1}^2}{z_t^3}  
        \right]
```

The cross-partial is

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial a \partial b} (a,b) 
    = 
    \sum_{t=1}^{T-1} x_t^2
    \left[
            \frac{1}{z_t^2} - 2 \frac{x_{t+1}^2}{z_t^3}  
        \right]
```

From these expressions we can form the gradient vector and the Hessian, pick an initial guess, and iterate according to {eq}`eq-2dnr`. {numref}`f-nr` shows four iterations of this procedure, starting from $(a_0, b_0) = (0.65, 0.35)$.[^5] In this case the convergence is quick, and we are already close to the global optimum.

````{solution} ex-avmsar

Let $\{x_t\}$ be as in the statement of the problem. The claim is that {eq}`eq-nsmcar` holds. To see this, apply {eq}`eq-mkclt`. (All the relevant conditions are satisfied; see, in particular, [example %s](#eg-ar1ssc). Observe also that the stationary distribution $\pi_{\infty} = \nN(0, 1/(1 - a^2))$ has finite moments of all orders.) This gives

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}} \sum_{t=1}^T x_t 
         = \sqrt{T} \left[
            \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T x_t 
                    \right]
         = \sqrt{T} \left[
            \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T x_t - \int s \pi_{\infty}(s) \diff s
                    \right]
        \tod \nN(0, v)
```

where, from {eq}`eq-mavr`,

```{math}
:enumerated: false

v = \var[x_0] + 2 \sum_{t=1}^{\infty} \cov [x_0, x_t]
```

Recall here that $\lL(x_0) = \nN(0, 1/(1-a^2))$, so this becomes

```{math}
:enumerated: false

v 
         = \frac{1}{1 - a^2} + 2 \sum_{t=1}^{\infty} a^t \EE [x_0^2 ]
         = \frac{1}{1 - a^2} + \frac{2}{1 - a^2} \sum_{t=1}^{\infty}
            a^t
         = \frac{1}{1 - a^2} + \frac{2a}{(1 - a)(1 - a^2)}
```

Here the first equality follows from the discussion of correlation in {ref}`ss-larlln`. Simplifying the right-hand side gives $v = 1/(1-a)^2$ as claimed.
````


````{solution} ex-avmsarv

In this exercise we are assuming that $x_{t+1} = a x_t + w_{t+1}$ where $|a| < 1$, $\{w_t\}$ is IID and standard normal, and $\lL(x_0) = \pi_{\infty} = \nN(0, 1/(1-a^2))$. (The stationary distribution was obtained.) The aim is to show that

$$
\sqrt{T}
        \left\{
            s_T^2 - \frac{1}{1 - a^2}
        \right\}
        \tod
        \nN (0, \gamma^2)
        \quad \text{where} \quad
        \gamma^2 := 2 \frac{1 + a^2}{(1 - a^2)^3}
$$ (eq-mansv)
Letting $v := 1 /(1 -a^2)$, note from the manipulations in [example %s](#eg-svcan) that it suffices to prove that

$$
\sqrt{T}
        \left\{
            \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T x_t^2 - v
        \right\}
        \tod
        \nN (0, \gamma^2)
$$ (eq-mansv2)
All of the conditions of [theorem %s](#t-umspt) are satisfied, and we apply the resulting CLT in {eq}`eq-mkclt` with $h(x) = x^2$ to get {eq}`eq-mansv2` with

$$
\gamma^2
        := \EE[(x_0^2 - v)^2] + 2 \sum_{t=1}^{\infty} \EE [(x_0^2 - v)(x_t^2 - v)]
$$ (eq-g2fm)
To check {eq}`eq-mansv` it only remains to show that this expression for $\gamma^2$ matches that in {eq}`eq-mansv`. To see this, consider the first term on the right-hand side of {eq}`eq-g2fm`. We have

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\EE[(x_0^2 - v)^2] 
        = \EE[x_0^4] - 2 v \EE[x_0^2 ] + v^2
        = 2 v^2
```

(The fourth moment of the distribution $N(0, \sigma^2)$ is $3 \sigma^4$.) For the second term on the right-hand side of {eq}`eq-g2fm`, we have

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\EE [(x_0^2 - v)(x_t^2 - v)]
        = \EE[x_0^2 x_t^2] - v^2
        = \EE\left\{ x_0^2 [ a^t x_0 + s(w)]^2 \right\} - v^2
```

for $s(w) := \sum_{i=1}^t a^{t-i} w_i$. Note that $s(w)$ and $x_0$ is independent. (For a Markov process, the initial condition is always assumed to be independent of the shocks.) Working through all the algebra, we get

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\EE [(x_0^2 - v)(x_t^2 - v)] = 2 a^{2t} v^2
```

Returning to {eq}`eq-g2fm` we have

$$
\gamma^2
        =  2 v^2 + 2 \sum_{t=1}^{\infty} 2 a^{2t} v^2
        =  2 v^2 + 4 \frac{a^2}{1 - a^2} v^2
$$
Using $v = 1/(1-a^2)$ and working through the remaining algebra will get you to the expression for $\gamma^2$ in {eq}`eq-mansv`.
````


````{solution} ex-chrtsc

Let $\pi_{\infty}(s) = 2 \phi(s) \Phi(q s)$, where $q := a (1 - a^2)^{-1/2}$, $\phi$ is the standard normal density and $\Phi$ is the standard normal CDF. The process $\{x_t\}$ studied in [example %s](#ex-chrtsc) is identically distributed as a result of our assumption that $\lL(x_0) = \pi_{\infty}$ (see [fact %s](#fa-idmp)). It remains to check the conditions on $\Sigmax$. In the present case,

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\Sigmax 
        = \EE[ x_t^2 ] 
        = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} s^2 \pi_{\infty}(s)\diff s
        = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} s^2 2 \phi(s) \Phi(qs) \diff s
```

To verify that $\Sigmax$ is positive definite, we need to check that the term on the right-hand side is strictly positive. This is clearly true, because the function inside the integral is strictly positive everywhere but zero. To be careful, we should also check that $\Sigmax$ is finite, and this is also true because $\Phi(qs) \leq 1$, and hence

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} s^2 2 \phi(s) \Phi(qs) \diff s
        \leq \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} s^2 2 \phi(s) \diff s
        = 2 \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} s^2 \phi(s) \diff s
        = 2
```

Last, we need to show that

$$
\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T x_t^2 \toprob 
            \Sigmax = \EE[ x_t^2 ]
$$ (eq-ztarll)
Since the conditions of [theorem %s](#t-umspt) are satisfied, the convergence in {eq}`eq-ztarll` is valid.
````

````{solution} ex-cstsc

Suppose that [assumption %s](#a-stsc) is valid. We need to show that

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\EE[ u_s u_t \given \boldx_1, \ldots,\boldx_t]  
        = 
        \begin{cases}
            & \sigma^2 \quad \text{if} \quad s = t \\ 
            & 0 \quad \;\; \text{if} \quad s < t 
        \end{cases}

On one hand, if $s = t$, then $\EE[ u_t^2 \given \boldx_1, \ldots,\boldx_t] = \EE[ u_t^2] = \sigma^2$ by independence. On the other hand, if $s < t$, then

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\begin{aligned}
\EE[ u_s u_t \given \boldx_1, \ldots,\boldx_t]  
        & = \EE[ \EE[ u_s u_t \given \boldx_1, \ldots,\boldx_t, u_s ]
                        \given \boldx_1, \ldots,\boldx_t]  \\
        & = \EE[ u_s \EE[ u_t \given \boldx_1, \ldots,\boldx_t, u_s ]
                        \given \boldx_1, \ldots,\boldx_t] \\ 
        & = \EE[ u_s \EE[ u_t ] \given \boldx_1, \ldots,\boldx_t]  \\
        & = \EE[ u_s 0 \given \boldx_1, \ldots,\boldx_t] = 0
        
\end{aligned}
```
````

````{solution} ex-btcan

By assumption we have $\hat \boldD_T \toprob \boldD$ and $\sqrt{T} (\hat \boldtheta_T - \boldtheta) \tod \boldz$, where $\lL(\boldz) = \nN(\boldzero, \boldC)$. Applying Slutsky's theorem, we obtain

\hat \boldD_T \sqrt{T} (\hat \boldtheta_T - \boldtheta) \tod \boldD \boldz

Clearly $\boldD \boldz$ is normally distributed with mean $\boldzero$. Moreover

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\var[ \boldD \boldz ] 
        = \boldD \var[ \boldz ] \boldD^\T
        = \boldD \boldC \boldD
        = \boldI
```

In other words, $\boldD \boldz$ is standard normal. As a result, $\| \boldD \boldz \|^2 \sim \chi^2(K)$. Applying the continuous mapping theorem to {eq}`eq-cdqz`, we obtain

```{math}
:enumerated: false

\| \hat \boldD_T \sqrt{T} (\hat \boldtheta_T - \boldtheta) \|^2
        \tod \| \boldD \boldz \|^2 \sim \chi^2(K)
```

This is equivalent to {eq}`eq-adqbt`.
````
